Localizing Microsoft’s typography for Chinese.

As a typography specialist, I partnered with MSN and Bing’s local design teams in China to develop multilingual font proposals and provide technical guidance.

01 / Understand the mismatch

Matching numbers can produce very different type.

Chinese text in Discover Feeds needed to work alongside Ginto. Using the same size, weight, and line height across scripts could leave Chinese headings looking heavy, paragraphs crowded, and mixed text unevenly aligned.

I examined weight, apparent character size, line height, letter spacing, and baseline alignment. Comparisons of characters, phrases, and paragraphs helped turn those visual differences into specific design decisions.

The original slides below come from the internal typography session I co-presented with Bingnan Jiang. I used these comparisons to explain the reasoning behind the proposals.

Read the shape inside the font size.

A font size describes the em, but the visible letterform occupies only part of that space. Latin x-height, capitals, and ascenders create a different rhythm from Chinese glyphs. I used these structural differences to explain why equal font sizes can still look mismatched.

Understand the available weight range.

The Microsoft YaHei version examined in the study offered Light, Regular, and Bold. Its available weights did not map neatly to Ginto’s finer weight range. That made weight availability a design constraint when balancing Chinese and Latin headlines.

02 / Choose the pairing

Choose the font and its settings together.

I explored adjustments to Microsoft YaHei, then compared it with Noto Sans SC alongside Ginto. The study considered whether a broader choice of weights and different glyph proportions could produce a more balanced pairing.

The design proposal used Noto Sans SC and considered different spacing needs for interface labels, summaries, and body text. The aim was to support both quick scanning and longer reading.

Compare apparent size and baseline together.

I compared Microsoft YaHei and Noto Sans SC in the same Chinese–Latin samples. The overlaid glyphs made differences in proportion visible; the mixed-text lines showed how each font sat beside Ginto. These comparisons informed the proposed pairing.

I also treated line height and tracking as context-dependent decisions. A short interface label, a scannable summary, and an article paragraph have different reading demands, so the proposal considered each separately.

03 / Compare in context

See how the type works across a whole page.

The typography proposals were illustrated in Discover Feeds and article layouts. The before-and-after designs show changes to card headings, source labels, article titles, body text, and Chinese–Latin text within the same line.

04 / Explain font fallback

Trace the font behind each character.

A font stack can render one headline with several typefaces. When the first font lacks a character, the browser looks for a fallback that can draw it. I broke a mixed Chinese–Latin headline down character by character to show which font was actually used.

In the configuration shown in the sharing deck, a requested weight of 550 produced Ginto at 550 and static Noto Sans SC at 600. The shared number in the style rule did not guarantee a shared visual weight.

Pair weights by how they read together.

The teaching example then compared Ginto at 460 with Noto Sans SC at 500. I used the pairing to make a practical point: inspect the rendered result and the weights each font provides, then tune the relationship between scripts. Those values describe this example, rather than a universal setting for every interface.

05 / Check language details

Make room for each writing system.

I extended the sharing beyond Chinese to show what can happen when a font change reaches other scripts. Thai and Telugu examples exposed lines touching or being cropped. They helped explain why teams need to inspect actual content after a font switch, including marks above and below the main letterforms.

Use the glyph forms expected in each locale.

CJK characters can share a Unicode code point while using different regional glyph forms. I compared Noto Sans SC and Noto Sans JP with characters such as 骨, 直, and 画, then explained language-specific font stacks. Correct character coverage is only part of the decision; the selected font must also suit the content’s language.

06 / Coordinate the switch

Help product teams take the proposals toward release.

I worked with MSN and Bing’s local design teams in China when they needed support with font updates. I developed multilingual font proposals they could implement and supported technical discussions and internal pitches.

I also worked with the Windows, Office, and Edge teams on Chinese font-switching plans using Noto Sans CN, supplied with Windows 11 and Edge.

I also joined technical discussions with FounderType about updates to Microsoft YaHei’s font weights.

I guided the teams as they moved the proposals toward release. Designers within each product team handled launch testing and the subsequent follow-up.

07 / Share the principles

Give designers a way to reason about multilingual type.

I shared multilingual typography principles with internal design teams and developed font fallback guidance. The material explained differences between writing systems, how browsers choose fonts for individual characters, and why the same weight value can look different across fonts.

The guidance also addressed regional CJK glyph forms and language-specific font choices. It gave teams concrete comparisons to use when evaluating typography in their own interfaces.
